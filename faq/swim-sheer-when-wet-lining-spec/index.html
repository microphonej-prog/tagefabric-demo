The swim fabric looked opaque on the swatch but the suit goes sheer in the water — what has to be specified?

Sportswear-specific · TAGE FABRIC technical note

Opacity on a dry swatch tells you almost nothing about a swimsuit. Dry, the gaps between yarns are filled with air and light scatters at every fiber surface. Soaked, water with a refractive index near 1.33 replaces that air, the scattering collapses, and the same knit transmits far more light. Stretch adds the second hit: a seat panel at 30–40% elongation has a lower cover factor than the flat square you approved.

So the property to specify is opacity wet and stretched, judged on a sewn garment rather than a cut swatch. A workable protocol: submerge the sample for two minutes, hold it over a mid-gray card under D65 light at the maximum stretch the finished pattern applies, and photograph it. Write the pass as “no hand or print visible through a single layer at 30% width stretch, in the lightest color in the range.”

There are three levers. Weight and density first — 190–230 gsm at a tight gauge reads far more solid than 160 gsm. Lining second, and this is where most programs solve it: a bonded power mesh or solid knit at the front gusset and bust, color-matched to the shell, costs a fraction of moving the whole fabric up a weight class. Color third, because white and pale pastels expose what black absorbs.

Do not skip the wet test because the dry swatch passed. It is the one garment property that only appears in use, and it appears in reviews.
